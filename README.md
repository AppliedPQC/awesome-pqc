# Awesome PQC [![Follow @AppliedPQC on X](https://img.shields.io/badge/X-%40AppliedPQC-000000?logo=x&logoColor=white)](https://x.com/AppliedPQC)

> A curated, **link-verified** list of post-quantum cryptography resources for
> people who have to *build* and *ship* it.

## Why this list

[`veorq/awesome-post-quantum`](https://github.com/veorq/awesome-post-quantum) is
the canonical general PQC list and is actively maintained — **start there** for
breadth, papers, and policy. This list is deliberately narrower and focuses on
three things: **implementation** (code, test vectors, conformance),
**industry deployment** (what is actually shipping in TLS, SSH, browsers, and
hardware), and **migration** (inventory, hybrid rollout, and timelines).

## Standards

### NIST

- [Post-Quantum Cryptography project](https://csrc.nist.gov/Projects/post-quantum-cryptography) — the authoritative status page for the whole programme.
- [FIPS 203: ML-KEM](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/fips/203/final) ([PDF](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/FIPS/NIST.FIPS.203.pdf)) — module-lattice KEM, from CRYSTALS-Kyber. Final, August 2024.
- [FIPS 204: ML-DSA](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/fips/204/final) ([PDF](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/FIPS/NIST.FIPS.204.pdf)) — module-lattice signatures, from CRYSTALS-Dilithium. Final, August 2024.
- [FIPS 205: SLH-DSA](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/fips/205/final) ([PDF](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/FIPS/NIST.FIPS.205.pdf)) — stateless hash-based signatures, from SPHINCS+. Final, August 2024.
- **FIPS 206: FN-DSA** (Falcon) — *in development* at NIST: the project page says standardization of Falcon and HQC "is underway", and the final IR 8547 says NIST is developing a FIPS derived from Falcon. The [round-3 Falcon submission](https://falcon-sign.info/) is the authoritative specification.
- [NIST IR 8547: Transition to Post-Quantum Cryptography Standards](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/ir/8547/final) ([PDF](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2026/NIST.IR.8547.pdf)) — the transition schedule. Final, October 2026. Every quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithm (RSA, ECDSA, EdDSA, finite-field and elliptic-curve Diffie-Hellman, MQV) is deprecated after 2030 and disallowed after 2035, at every key size; the 2024 draft deprecated only the 112-bit ones. Recommends ML-KEM-768 as the default, and accepts hybrid key establishment and dual signatures in FIPS 140 validation as long as one component is approved, even if the other is disallowed.
- [SP 800-208: Stateful Hash-Based Signatures](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/208/final) — approves LMS and XMSS for narrow use (firmware signing). Stateful: key reuse is catastrophic.
- [SP 800-57 Part 1 Rev. 6: Recommendation for Key Management](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/57/pt1/r6/ipd) — initial public draft, 5 December 2025; comments closed 5 February 2026. NIST's general key-management guidance, covering key types, their protection and key-management functions, brings in the quantum-resistant algorithms of FIPS 203, 204 and 205 together with the security categories of the PQC competition, treats key-establishment and key-storage keys separately, and replaces its own algorithm-approval time frames with references to SP 800-131A.
- [SP 800-52 Rev. 2: TLS guidelines, to be revised](https://csrc.nist.gov/News/2026/nist-will-revise-tls-guidelines) — on 19 November 2026 NIST announced that it will revise its 2019 guidelines for selecting, configuring and using TLS, after a review that took public comments in May 2026. The revision will add support for post-quantum algorithms, align with TLS 1.3 as now specified in RFC 9846, say servers "may" rather than "should" support TLS 1.2, and deprecate or disallow TLS 1.2 cipher suites with SHA-1, DSA, static key exchange or RSA key transport; the CMVP will define a timeline after which a module needs a strong justification to include TLS 1.2.
- [FIPS 140-3 Implementation Guidance](https://csrc.nist.gov/projects/cryptographic-module-validation-program/fips-140-3-ig-announcements) — the CMVP's rules for validating cryptographic modules, which decide whether a hybrid post-quantum construction can pass FIPS 140-3 at all. The 27 February 2026 update added KEMs to the acceptable key-establishment protocols in D.A; on 9 April 2026 D.S gained guidance for SP 800-227, including a new scenario for hybrid KEMs; the 19 August 2026 version adds a paragraph to the self-test guidance in 10.3.A on key pairs generated from stored seeds, the storage form FIPS 203 and FIPS 204 allow; and the 20 November 2026 version updates D.S to cite the published RFC 10024 in place of the hybrid TLS 1.3 draft and to accept the TLS 1.3 KDF CVL as a valid key combiner in the TLS 1.3 hybrid protocol, so a module's X25519MLKEM768 handshake has a named, testable combiner.
- [Working drafts: post-quantum updates to the PIV standards](https://csrc.nist.gov/news/2026/pqc-updates-to-piv-standards-working-drafts) — June 2026: SP 800-73-6 Parts 1 and 2 and SP 800-78-6, the smart-card standards behind US federal identity badges, with ML-DSA signatures and ML-KEM key establishment added beside the classical keys rather than replacing them, so one card carries both. Published as working drafts at [pages.nist.gov/piv-standards](https://pages.nist.gov/piv-standards/) with comments through a [GitHub repository](https://github.com/usnistgov/piv-standards) and no closing date; NIST calls them preliminary material, not a formal public draft. The first place NIST spells out which key references, containers and card commands a post-quantum credential needs.
- [Additional Digital Signature Schemes](https://csrc.nist.gov/projects/pqc-dig-sig) — the signature "on-ramp", seeking non-lattice diversity. [NIST IR 8610](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/ir/8610/final) (May 2026) moves nine schemes to a third round: FAEST, HAWK, MAYO, MQOM, QR-UOV, SDitH, SNOVA, SQIsign and UOV. After recent cryptanalysis NIST expects a longer road for the four multivariate ones (MAYO, QR-UOV, SNOVA, UOV) and is unlikely to standardize any of them without a further round; the next standardization conference is planned for the first half of 2027. HAWK, the only lattice-based candidate, has since [been withdrawn](https://csrc.nist.gov/Projects/pqc-dig-sig/round-3-additional-signatures) by its team, on 29 July 2026, leaving eight: a [key-recovery attack posted to pqc-forum](https://groups.google.com/a/list.nist.gov/g/pqc-forum/c/2r2u6SbHun4/m/0_I2KOZ_CQAJ) the day before reduces HAWK-n key recovery to SVP in dimension n/2 + 1, lowering key recovery from 2^150 to 2^108 for HAWK-512 and from 2^288 to 2^182 for HAWK-1024 in the AGPS'20 gate-count model, and states that Falcon and ML-DSA are not affected. NIST [notes](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2026/05/nine-candidates-advance-third-round-additional-digital-signatures-pqc) Anthropic's announcement that its AI model found the attack; the [attack code](https://github.com/anthropics/cryptography-research-demo/tree/main/HAWK) (Apache-2.0, an unmaintained research artifact) recovers a working HAWK-256 key in about 3 h 42 min on one 96-core server.

### IETF

- [PQUIP working group](https://datatracker.ietf.org/wg/pquip/about/) — post-quantum use in protocols.
- [RFC 9958](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9958.html) — *Post-Quantum Cryptography for Engineers*, PQUIP's account of what a cryptographically relevant quantum computer breaks and what the transition demands of deployed systems (June 2026).
- [draft-ietf-pquip-pqc-hsm-constrained](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-pquip-pqc-hsm-constrained/) — *Adapting Constrained Devices for Post-Quantum Cryptography* (Informational): PQUIP's guidance on integrating PQC into resource-constrained devices. Approved by the IESG and in the RFC Editor queue since 2026-11-19.
- [`ietf-wg-pquip/state-of-protocols-and-pqc`](https://github.com/ietf-wg-pquip/state-of-protocols-and-pqc) — a map of which IETF protocols have PQC stories. Useful, but last updated June 2025, so check dates against the drafts themselves.
- [RFC 9881](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9881.html) — X.509 algorithm identifiers for **ML-DSA** (October 2025).
- [RFC 9935](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9935.html) — X.509 algorithm identifiers for **ML-KEM** (March 2026).
- [RFC 9909](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9909.html) — X.509 algorithm identifiers for **SLH-DSA** (December 2025).
- [RFC 9814](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9814.html), [RFC 9882](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9882.html) and [RFC 9936](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9936.html) — SLH-DSA (July 2025), ML-DSA (October 2025) and ML-KEM (March 2026) in the Cryptographic Message Syntax: the signed-data and enveloped-data formats under S/MIME, code signing and document signing, where a certificate identifier alone is not enough.
- [RFC 9629](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9629.html) — KEM algorithms in the Cryptographic Message Syntax (August 2024, Standards Track, updating RFC 5652): the `KEMRecipientInfo` structure that lets enveloped data be encrypted to a key-encapsulation public key at all. RFC 9936 above only plugs ML-KEM into it, so a CMS library needs this one first.
- [RFC 9883](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9883.html) and [RFC 9810](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9810.html) — enrolment for KEM certificates, whose keys cannot sign their own request. RFC 9883 (October 2025, Standards Track) defines an attribute for PKCS#10 and CRMF requests in which the requester states possession of the private key, validated with a signature certificate already issued to the same subject; its example pairs an ML-DSA signature certificate with an ML-KEM key-establishment certificate. RFC 9810 (July 2025, Standards Track, obsoleting RFC 4210 and RFC 9480) adds management of certificates containing a KEM public key to the Certificate Management Protocol, with KEM keys supported for proof of possession and message protection, and introduces CMP version 3 for EnvelopedData.
- [RFC 9763](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9763.html) — related certificates for multiple authentications within a protocol (June 2025, Standards Track, from NSA authors): a `RelatedCertificate` extension, holding the hash of a single end-entity certificate, through which a CA signals that a new certificate is related to an existing one so the two can be used together. The RFC places it within a migration to PQC by non-composite hybrid authentication: a classical and a post-quantum certificate side by side, with no composite key or signature format.
- [RFC 9964](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9964.html) — ML-DSA for JOSE and COSE (May 2026): JSON and CBOR signatures, so tokens and IoT attestations have a post-quantum signature algorithm registered rather than improvised.
- [RFC 9980](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9980.html) — post-quantum cryptography in OpenPGP (June 2026): ML-KEM and ML-DSA composites with elliptic-curve algorithms, and SLH-DSA on its own.
- [RFC 9794](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9794.html) — terminology for post-quantum/traditional hybrid schemes (June 2025, Informational). Short, and the reason the RFCs above say "PQ/T hybrid" rather than six different things.
- [RFC 9954](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9954.html) — hybrid key exchange in TLS 1.3 (July 2026, Informational): the generic construction behind each hybrid group, with component keys and ciphertexts concatenated in a fixed order and shared secrets fed into the key schedule. It selects no algorithm, so implementers of a new hybrid group should follow it rather than invent their own combiner.
- [RFC 9955](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9955.html) — hybrid signature spectrums (July 2026, Informational): defines how hard it is to strip one component signature from a hybrid signature without detection, and how many approved modules a hybrid needs. Read it before choosing a hybrid signature for a FIPS-validated product.
- [RFC 8554](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8554.html) — LMS hash-based signatures.
- [RFC 8391](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8391.html) — XMSS, eXtended Merkle Signature Scheme.
- [RFC 9858](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9858.html) — additional HSS/LMS parameter sets (October 2025): the SHA-256/192 and SHAKE variants, including the shorter signatures SP 800-208 allows.
- [RFC 10033](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc10033.html) — state and backup management for LMS, HSS, XMSS and XMSS^MT (September 2026). The operational half of stateful signatures, where double-signing with one OTS key allows forgeries; read it before deploying either of the two above.
- [RFC 8778](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8778.html) — HSS/LMS in COSE (April 2020, Standards Track), registering the HSS-LMS algorithm and key type, with software update distribution as its stated use.
- [RFC 10024](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc10024.html) — the hybrid TLS 1.3 groups actually deployed today: X25519MLKEM768, SecP256r1MLKEM768 and SecP384r1MLKEM1024 (August 2026, formerly draft-ietf-tls-ecdhe-mlkem).
- [draft-ietf-tls-mlkem](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-tls-mlkem/) — standalone ML-KEM in the TLS 1.3 key schedule. Approved and in the RFC Editor queue since 2026-09-08.
- [draft-ietf-tls-mldsa](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-tls-mldsa/) — *Use of ML-DSA in TLS 1.3* (Informational): how ML-DSA is used for authentication in TLS 1.3, with the signature schemes `mldsa44`, `mldsa65` and `mldsa87` at code points 0x0904 to 0x0906. Approved by the IESG on 2026-09-10 and in the RFC Editor queue since 2026-09-11.
- [RFC 10042](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc10042.html) — hybrid ML-KEM key exchange for SSH, including the `mlkem768x25519-sha256` method OpenSSH uses by default (August 2026).
- [draft-ietf-ipsecme-ikev2-mlkem](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-ipsecme-ikev2-mlkem/) and [draft-ietf-ipsecme-ikev2-pqc-auth](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-ipsecme-ikev2-pqc-auth/) — ML-KEM for the multiple key exchanges of RFC 9370, and post-quantum signature authentication, both Proposed Standards approved by the IESG and in the RFC Editor queue. The first (approved 2026-07-07) specifies ML-KEM in IKEv2 by itself or as an additional key exchange along with a traditional one; the second (approved 2026-08-24, in the queue since 2026-08-25) is a generic mechanism for post-quantum signature authentication in IKEv2, with ML-DSA and SLH-DSA as authentication methods.
- [draft-ietf-ipsecme-ikev2-downgrade-prevention](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-ipsecme-ikev2-downgrade-prevention/) — *Downgrade Prevention for the Internet Key Exchange Protocol Version 2 (IKEv2)*, a Proposed Standard updating RFC 7296, by authors from ELVIS-PLUS and Cloudflare; approved by the IESG on 2026-07-06 and in the RFC Editor queue since 2026-07-07. Peers authenticate the full IKE_SA_INIT transcript, negotiated with the `IKE_SA_INIT_FULL_TRANSCRIPT_AUTH` notification, so an attacker able to break a traditional key exchange in real time cannot steer two peers away from the hybrid or pure post-quantum exchange both support. The protection holds when both peers implement the extension and at least one authentication credential is uncompromised, and the draft's own analysis says the coexistence of old and new algorithms during the post-quantum migration makes these attacks more relevant.
- [RFC 10073](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc10073.html) — composite ML-DSA for X.509 PKI (November 2026, Standards Track): ML-DSA-44, -65 or -87 paired with RSASSA-PSS, RSASSA-PKCS1-v1_5, ECDSA, Ed25519 or Ed448 under 18 registered identifiers, so hybrid certificates no longer need vendor-specific OIDs.
- [RFC 10082](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc10082.html) — composite ML-DSA in the Cryptographic Message Syntax (November 2026, Standards Track): the companion to RFC 10073, carrying the same 18 identifiers into signed data, so S/MIME, code signing and document signing can use a hybrid signature under a hybrid certificate instead of stopping at the certificate.
- [draft-ietf-lamps-pq-composite-kem](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-lamps-pq-composite-kem/) — composite ML-KEM for X.509 PKI, the key-establishment counterpart to RFC 10073: ML-KEM in hybrid with RSA-OAEP, ECDH, X25519 or X448, under twelve registered identifiers. A Proposed Standard, approved by the IESG and in the RFC Editor queue since 2026-10-12. Its companion for the Cryptographic Message Syntax, [draft-ietf-lamps-cms-composite-kem](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-lamps-cms-composite-kem/), also a Proposed Standard, was approved by the IESG on 2026-09-18 and entered the queue the same day: it carries the composite ML-KEM algorithms in the `KEMRecipientInfo` structure of RFC 9629, so enveloped data can be encrypted to a hybrid key and not only certified under one.
- CNSA 2.0 protocol profiles — six Informational drafts on the Independent Submission stream, by NSA authors, sent to the RFC Editor between 29 October and 4 November 2026: [TLS 1.3](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-becker-cnsa2-tls-profile/), [SSH](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-becker-cnsa2-ssh-profile/), [IPsec](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-guthrie-cnsa2-ipsec-profile/), [certificates and CRLs](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-jenkins-cnsa2-pkix-profile/), [S/MIME](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-becker-cnsa2-smime-profile/) and [CMC](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-jenkins-cnsa2-cmc-profile/). They turn the CNSA 2.0 algorithm list into wire-level requirements for US national security systems, and say of themselves that they are not IETF standards. TLS: version 1.3 only, with `TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384`, MLKEM1024 (0x0202) for key establishment and ML-DSA-87 (0x0906) as the only allowed signature algorithm, in certificates and CertificateVerify alike. SSH: ML-KEM-1024 is the only key exchange that may be negotiated, under a `mlkem1024-sha384` method the draft defines in its own appendix, with ML-DSA-87 host and user keys. IPsec: IKE_SA_INIT keeps a CNSA 1.0 group (384-bit ECP, or 3072- or 4096-bit MODP) because of the size of ML-KEM-1024's public key and ciphertext, a single RFC 9370 additional key exchange in IKE_INTERMEDIATE must be ML-KEM-1024, and authentication is ML-DSA-87. Certificates and CRLs are signed with ML-DSA-87 and carry an ML-DSA-87 or ML-KEM-1024 subject key. Five are written to obsolete their CNSA 1.0 predecessors: RFC 9206 (IPsec), RFC 9212 (SSH), RFC 8603 (certificates), RFC 8755 (S/MIME) and RFC 8756 (CMC). The place to read what an ML-KEM-1024 and ML-DSA-87 deployment has to put on the wire, pure except for the classical exchange IPsec keeps in IKE_SA_INIT.
- [draft-ietf-plants-merkle-tree-certs](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-plants-merkle-tree-certs/) — Merkle Tree Certificates, the PLANTS working group's Standards Track draft (-08, 2 November 2026), by authors from Google, Apple, Cloudflare and Geomys. The CA logs every certificate in a Merkle tree and cosigners sign its checkpoints, so transparency is part of issuance. A standalone certificate carries those cosignatures; a landmark-relative one replaces them with an inclusion proof to a subtree the browser already holds, 736 bytes with no signatures in the draft's example, against 2,420 bytes for one ML-DSA-44 signature. The example allocates a landmark every hour for certificates of up to 7 days. Chrome's root store, Let's Encrypt and Cloudflare build on it.

### National and regional guidance

- [ANSSI (France) — PQC FAQ](https://cyber.gouv.fr/enjeux-technologiques/cryptographie-post-quantique/faq-pqc/) — the agency's own words on its two dates: PQC obligations for products entering *qualification* from 2027, and "not reasonable" to buy products without PQC after 2030, with hybrid protection insisted on wherever post-quantum protection is needed. In French; the [2022 position paper](https://cyber.gouv.fr/en/publications/anssi-views-post-quantum-cryptography-transition) is in English. The rules behind it are in version 3.00 of [ANSSI-PG-083](https://messervices.cyber.gouv.fr/documents-guides/anssi-guide-mecanismes-crypto-3.00.pdf) (20 March 2026), the guide to choosing and sizing cryptographic mechanisms, which now takes the quantum threat into account. It recommends post-quantum security for any mechanism used beyond 1 January 2030 or exposed to retroactive attack. ML-KEM, from 512 up with 768 preferred, and FrodoKEM comply only when hybridised with a classical mechanism, as does ML-DSA, while SLH-DSA can be used as is after 2030. Three technical sheets of 2 February 2026 apply this to [TLS 1.3](https://messervices.cyber.gouv.fr/documents-guides/transition_post_quantique_tls_1_3.pdf), [SSHv2](https://messervices.cyber.gouv.fr/documents-guides/transition_post_quantique_ssh_v2.pdf) and [IPsec](https://messervices.cyber.gouv.fr/documents-guides/transition_post_quantique_ipsec.pdf), recommending hybridisation for both key exchange and signature authentication. On 17 November 2026 ANSSI published its [guide to implementing IPsec with post-quantum cryptography for *Diffusion Restreinte* data](https://messervices.cyber.gouv.fr/guides/implementation-ipsec-pqc-dr), which succeeds its IPsec DR documents and is aimed mainly at developers of products seeking qualification. It covers confidentiality and leaves post-quantum authentication out of scope, and its strongSwan 6.1.0 configurations are teaching examples, not a product recommendation.
- [ASD (Australia) — planning for post-quantum cryptography](https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/planning-for-post-quantum-cryptography) — the earliest blanket deadline listed here: ASD recommends ceasing all use of traditional asymmetric cryptography by the end of 2030. The [ISM's cryptography guidelines](https://www.cyber.gov.au/ism/2026-09/guidelines-for-cryptography) (September 2026 edition) make that concrete: RSA, DH, ECDH and ECDSA are not approved beyond 2030, and nor are ML-KEM-768 and ML-DSA-65, so new equipment and libraries must support ML-KEM-1024, ML-DSA-87, SHA-384, SHA-512 and AES-256 by 2030. Hybrids are neither recommended nor prohibited.
- [BSI (Germany) — quantum technologies and PQC](https://www.bsi.bund.de/EN/Themen/Unternehmen-und-Organisationen/Informationen-und-Empfehlungen/Quantentechnologien-und-Post-Quanten-Kryptografie/quantentechnologien-und-post-quanten-kryptografie_node.html) — recommendations that differ from NIST in useful ways, notably on hybrid modes. All four parts of [TR-02102](https://www.bsi.bund.de/EN/Themen/Unternehmen-und-Organisationen/Standards-und-Zertifizierung/Technische-Richtlinien/TR-nach-Thema-sortiert/tr02102/tr02102_node.html) (mechanisms and key lengths, TLS, IPsec, SSH) are at version 2026-01, and the TR page says the RFC 10024 groups SecP256r1MLKEM768 and SecP384r1MLKEM1024 will be recommended in the next TR-02102-2 and may already be used TR-compliant, with X25519MLKEM768 also permitted. With that edition BSI [set end dates](https://www.bsi.bund.de/DE/Service-Navi/Presse/Pressemitteilungen/Presse2026/260211_Ende_klassischer_Verschluesselungsverfahren.html) (11 February 2026): classical asymmetric encryption should no longer be used on its own after the end of 2031, or 2030 for the most sensitive applications, but combined with PQC in hybrid form, and sole use of classical signatures is planned to be discontinued by the end of 2035.
- [BSI — notes on Classic McEliece](https://www.bsi.bund.de/DE/Service-Navi/Presse/Alle-Meldungen-News/Meldungen/2026/Classic-McEliece_261001.html) ([English PDF](https://www.bsi.bund.de/SharedDocs/Downloads/EN/BSI/Crypto/Notes_Classic_McEliece.pdf?__blob=publicationFile&v=2)) — 1 October 2026: after this year's cryptanalytic progress BSI recommends against Classic McEliece for new developments, while stating that no practical attack exists on the TR-02102-1 parameter sets, that a hybrid combination still gives at least the classical scheme's security, and that ML-KEM and FrodoKEM are unaffected and now preferred, with HQC to follow once standardized. The first reversal of a national PQC recommendation; the cost estimates behind it are in [ePrint 2026/1984](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/1984), 2^94 to 2^102 bit operations in the GIJS model against a claimed 2^207, demonstrated only at toy size.
- [Canadian Centre for Cyber Security — ITSP.40.111, cryptographic algorithms for UNCLASSIFIED, PROTECTED A and PROTECTED B information](https://cyber.gc.ca/en/guidance/cryptographic-algorithms-unclassified-protected-and-protected-b-information-itsp40111) — version 5, in effect from 29 May 2026: ML-KEM-512, -768 and -1024 recommended for general-purpose key establishment and ML-DSA-44, -65 and -87 for signatures, all twelve SLH-DSA parameter sets, and LMS, HSS, XMSS and XMSS^MT per SP 800-208. RSA, finite-field and elliptic-curve Diffie-Hellman and MQV, ECDSA and EdDSA used without a post-quantum scheme are to be phased out by the end of 2035; by the end of 2030 RSA moduli must be at least 3072 bits and DSA is phased out.
- [CRYPTREC (Japan) — Ciphers List](https://www.cryptrec.go.jp/en/list.html) — the cipher lists behind Japan's e-Government procurement. The revision of 30 March 2026 (CRYPTREC LS-0001-2022R2) added ML-KEM for key exchange to the Recommended Ciphers List, as CRYPTREC's [update notice](https://www.cryptrec.go.jp/en/) records. The technical background is the [Post-Quantum Cryptography guideline, 2024 edition](https://www.cryptrec.go.jp/report/cryptrec-gl-2007-2024.pdf) (CRYPTREC GL-2007-2024, March 2025).
- [CSA (Singapore) — advisory on quantum-safe migration for organisations](https://www.csa.gov.sg/alerts-and-advisories/advisories/advisory-on-quantum-safe-migration-for-organisations/) — 14 October 2026: owners of Critical Information Infrastructure should complete migration by 2031, and other organisations exposed to quantum risk should have a migration plan by the end of 2027 and complete migration by 2035. The [announcement](https://www.csa.gov.sg/news-events/press-releases/csa-sets-out-quantum-safe-migration-timeline-for-organisations-and-launches-new-grant/) adds a Quantum-Safe Kickstarter Grant, open from 1 November 2026 to 31 October 2027, funding pilots of up to six months, with cryptographic discovery and inventory and the migration of OT and legacy systems among its priority use cases.
- [EU — Coordinated Implementation Roadmap for the Transition to Post-Quantum Cryptography](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/coordinated-implementation-roadmap-transition-post-quantum-cryptography) — the NIS Cooperation Group's roadmap (June 2025): national strategies and inventories started by end-2026, high-risk and critical-infrastructure use cases migrated by 2030, the rest by 2035. The Commission's [proposal to amend NIS2](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-5627-2026-INIT/en/pdf) (COM(2026) 13, 20 January 2026) would require each Member State's national cybersecurity strategy to cover the transition to post-quantum cryptography, with its recital naming the same 2030 and 2035 targets.
- [EU — ECCG Agreed Cryptographic Mechanisms](https://certification.enisa.europa.eu/publications/eucc-guidelines-cryptography_en) — the mechanisms products should use to pass EUCC certification, aimed at developers and evaluators. Version 2 (6 May 2025) recommends ML-KEM, FrodoKEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA (security levels 3 and 5 only), LMS and XMSS, with the largest parameter sets preferred, and says (M)LWE-based mechanisms should not be used standalone. The version 3 draft (public review from 2 June to the end of July 2026) adds the RFC 10024 TLS 1.3 groups X25519MLKEM768, SecP256r1MLKEM768 and SecP384r1MLKEM1024 and the SP 800-227 KEM combiners as recommended. The same page carries ENISA's report *Hybridization of traditional cryptographic mechanisms with PQC: Standardisation Status* (30 April 2026).
- [G7 Cyber Security Working Group — statement on preparing for a post-quantum cryptography migration](https://www.cyber.gc.ca/en/news-events/g7-cybersecurity-working-group-statement-preparing-post-quantum-cryptography-migration) — June 2026, from Canada's CSE, Germany's BSI, Italy's ACN, France's ANSSI, the US CISA, the UK's NCSC and DSIT and Japan's NCO, in collaboration with the European Commission: it expects migration in the private sector to be completed during the 2030s. The [call to action](https://cyber.gouv.fr/nous-connaitre/publications/publications-internationales/preparing-for-the-post-quantum-era-a-call-to-action/) that followed on 3 September 2026 sets five priorities: raising awareness of quantum risks, national strategies that support PQC adoption, research and development for quantum-safe technologies, public-private partnerships, and integrating PQC into cybersecurity requirements and procurement. The common ground under the national timelines in this section, which differ on dates and on hybrids.
- [NCSC (UK) — next steps in preparing for post-quantum cryptography](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/paper/next-steps-in-preparing-for-post-quantum-cryptography) — the algorithm choices behind the timeline below (version 2.0, August 2024): ML-KEM-768 and ML-DSA-65 for most use cases, a PQ/T hybrid only as an interim measure inside a framework that can move to PQC-only, hash-based signatures for firmware and software signing, and LMS or XMSS only where state can be managed in a trusted manner.
- [NCSC (UK) — timelines for migration to post-quantum cryptography](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/guidance/pqc-migration-timelines) — discovery and plan by 2028, highest-priority migrations by 2031, complete by 2035 (March 2025). Prefers a single move to pure PQC over hybrids, which is where it parts from BSI and ANSSI.
- [NCSC (UK) — using TLS to protect data](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/guidance/using-tls-to-protect-data) — updated 15 October 2026 with post-quantum configurations ([announcement](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blogs/post-quantum-updates-to-our-tls-guidance)): the hybrid groups X25519MLKEM768, SecP256r1MLKEM768 and SecP384r1MLKEM1024 are recommended, pure MLKEM768 is a recommended profile with MLKEM1024 acceptable, and clients should offer X25519MLKEM768 and secp256r1. Hybrids are called an interim measure, which is how the pure-PQC preference above survives in a document that recommends them. Authentication stays classical, ECDSA P-256 or RSA-2048, until post-quantum TLS authentication is standardised, and certificates should not outlive your planned migration date. TLS 1.3 only; older versions will not get PQC.
- [US — Executive Order 14412](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/06/securing-the-nation-against-advanced-cryptographic-attacks/) and [OMB M-26-15](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/M-26-15-Execution-of-the-Migration-to-Post-Quantum-Cryptography.pdf) — June 2026. Federal high value assets, high impact systems and other highly sensitive systems must use PQC key establishment by 31 December 2030 and PQC signatures by 31 December 2031, with the rest by 2035; each agency's migration plan was due within 120 days, inventories feed a cryptographic bill of materials, and TLS 1.3 support is required by 2 January 2030. Systems that cannot support PQC are to be replaced or decommissioned. The memo makes NIST IR 8547 the schedule agencies must align with.
- **NSA CNSA 2.0**, **CISA Quantum Readiness**, and **NIST NCCoE migration** guidance are primary migration sources, but `nsa.gov`, `cisa.gov` and `nccoe.nist.gov` return HTTP 403 to automated clients, so their URLs are not listed here as verified links. Reach them from the [NIST PQC project page](https://csrc.nist.gov/Projects/post-quantum-cryptography) or [veorq's list](https://github.com/veorq/awesome-post-quantum#migration-guidelines). NSA's 1 October 2026 announcement restates the CNSA 2.0 timeline as reported: new national security system acquisitions quantum-resistant from 2027, legacy systems phased out by 2030. Version 2.2 of the CNSA 2.0 FAQ (October 2026) puts dates on each step: in 2027 NIAP stops accepting products for certification that have not started their quantum transition, NSS equipment and services that cannot support CNSA 2.0 must be phased out by 31 December 2030, and CNSA 2.0 algorithms (ML-KEM-1024, ML-DSA-87, and LMS or XMSS for firmware and software signing) are mandated by 31 December 2031. NSA does not require hybrid certified products. CISA's *Product Categories for Technologies That Use Post-Quantum Cryptography Standards* (23 January 2026, written under Executive Order 14306 in consultation with NSA) names the hardware and software categories, such as cloud services, web software and endpoint security, where PQC is widely available or arriving; CISA's own [GovDelivery bulletin](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USDHSCISA/bulletins/405c246) announcing it does resolve, and carries the `cisa.gov` link.

## Test vectors and conformance

The part most implementers underestimate. An implementation is not correct because it round-trips with itself.

- [`usnistgov/ACVP-Server`](https://github.com/usnistgov/ACVP-Server) — NIST's ACVP vectors. The `gen-val/json-files/` tree holds `internalProjection.json` files carrying **both inputs and expected outputs** for `ML-KEM-keyGen-FIPS203`, `ML-KEM-encapDecap-FIPS203`, `ML-DSA-{keyGen,sigGen,sigVer}-FIPS204` and `SLH-DSA-{keyGen,sigGen,sigVer}-FIPS205`. This is the ground truth for byte-exact conformance. Release v1.1.0.43 (tagged 2026-08-12) adds `-tr1` sets for `ML-KEM-encapDecap` and `ML-DSA-sigGen` that exercise both private-key formats, seed and expanded, which matters for any implementation that stores only the seed. The same release added LMS `sigGen` and `sigVer` vectors for SP 800-208, on the production server since 2026-08-20.
- [Falcon round-3 KATs](https://falcon-sign.info/) — the submission package ships `KAT/falcon{512,1024}-KAT.rsp`. Since FIPS 206 has no vectors, these are the only known-answer tests for FN-DSA.
- [`mupq/pqm4`](https://github.com/mupq/pqm4) — PQC on Cortex-M4, with testing and benchmarking harnesses.

- [`pq-crystals/dilithium`](https://github.com/pq-crystals/dilithium) — reference and optimised ML-DSA ([site](https://pq-crystals.org/dilithium/)).

- [HQC](https://pqc-hqc.org/) — code-based KEM, selected March 2025 as a backup to ML-KEM with different hardness assumptions. NIST IR 8547 says it becomes an approved scheme once its FIPS is published, and that no further KEMs are planned.
- [`pq-code-package`](https://github.com/pq-code-package) — the Post-Quantum Cryptography Alliance's high-assurance implementations, [`mlkem-native`](https://github.com/pq-code-package/mlkem-native) and [`mldsa-native`](https://github.com/pq-code-package/mldsa-native): portable C90 ML-KEM and ML-DSA, written for integration into other libraries. `mlkem-native`'s AArch64 and x86_64 assembly is proved correct and constant-time in HOL-Light, and it ships inside AWS-LC. PQClean's retirement notice names both as its successors. Both reached v2.0.0 on 7 August 2026; for `mldsa-native` it is the [first stable release](https://github.com/pq-code-package/mldsa-native/releases/tag/v2.0.0), with every routine of its AArch64 and x86_64 backends proved functionally correct in HOL Light, all but the secret-vector rejection samplers proved to run in secret-independent time, and APIs not marked experimental kept stable until v3.

Bindings: [Python](https://github.com/open-quantum-safe/liboqs-python), [Rust](https://github.com/open-quantum-safe/liboqs-rust), [Go](https://github.com/open-quantum-safe/liboqs-go).

### General-purpose crypto libraries with PQC

- [`openssl/openssl`](https://github.com/openssl/openssl) — native ML-KEM, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA since [3.5](https://openssl-library.org/post/2025-04-08-openssl-35-final-release/); 4.0 (April 2026) adds the `curveSM2MLKEM768` hybrid group and the ML-DSA-MU digest, and [4.1](https://openssl-library.org/post/2026-10-23-openssl-41-final-release/) (October 2026) adds no new post-quantum algorithms but optimises the existing ones: ML-KEM and ML-DSA on ppc64le, ML-DSA on x86_64.
- [`aws/aws-lc`](https://github.com/aws/aws-lc) — AWS's BoringSSL fork, FIPS-validated branches. [v5.9.0](https://github.com/aws/aws-lc/releases/tag/v5.9.0) (September 2026) adds ML-KEM to its HPKE, following draft-ietf-hpke-pq-05, and adopts OpenSSL's long names for the ML-DSA OIDs, so keys move between the two libraries without renaming.
- [`google/boringssl`](https://github.com/google/boringssl) — where Chrome's ML-KEM lives, and now its ML-DSA and SLH-DSA: October 2026 commits [limit ML-DSA advertising to TLS 1.3](https://github.com/google/boringssl/commit/e2cc86c0c08a2f5a4421ccfa8d3e50d6f797e216) and add [EVP_PKEY and SPKI/PKCS#8 support for SLH-DSA per RFC 9909](https://github.com/google/boringssl/commit/5aa397a5b69b2762ce3fa1084a9caf75fece3936), and November 2026 moves its Merkle Tree Certificate code to draft-07, [dropping draft-05](https://github.com/google/boringssl/commit/fc6504a623d8cf5b92fafec714ac28396f67c01b) because Chrome no longer configures such CAs. On 19 November 2026 it [removed `X509_V_FLAG_USE_MTC_DRAFT_PLANTS_07`](https://github.com/google/boringssl/commit/3b3a7aa0dece96d9f92c69e55ac4ae725d723d4b): draft-07 allocated stable OIDs, so `X509_verify_cert` now verifies every certificate from a CA carrying the MTC CA extension as a Merkle Tree Certificate, with no flag, and code that sets the flag fails to compile. Its dated releases, such as [0.20261105.0](https://github.com/google/boringssl/releases/tag/0.20261105.0) (5 November 2026), come with no guarantee of API or ABI stability, and the project does not recommend that third parties depend on it, so read the commit log.
- [`bcgit/bc-java`](https://github.com/bcgit/bc-java) — Bouncy Castle, the most complete JVM PQC support. [1.87](https://www.bouncycastle.org/resources/new-release-bouncy-castle-java-1-87/) (11 November 2026) puts X25519MLKEM768 first among the BCJSSE default TLS groups, implements RFC 9980's post-quantum OpenPGP, adds the eleven post-quantum MLS cipher suites of draft-ietf-mls-pq-ciphersuites under placeholder private-use code points, follows draft-ietf-hpke-pq-05 in HPKE, exposes composite ML-KEM through `javax.crypto.KEM` on Java 21 and later, and moves HQC to the team's 2026-10-12 specification, which breaks compatibility with earlier keys and ciphertexts. It also deprecates Classic McEliece, which the release says is no longer believed safe to use. The C# port, [`bcgit/bc-csharp`](https://github.com/bcgit/bc-csharp), added hybrid ECDHE-MLKEM key shares, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA to its TLS 1.3 in 2.7.0 (30 July 2026).
- [`randombit/botan`](https://github.com/randombit/botan) — C++ with broad PQC coverage, and hybrid X25519/ML-KEM-768 key exchange on by default in TLS, for clients and servers, since 3.7.0 (4 February 2025). [3.14.0](https://botan.randombit.net/news.html) (4 November 2026) accepts the FIPS 204 context string for ML-DSA, encodes ML-DSA and ML-KEM private keys per RFC 9881 and RFC 9935 while still reading the older formats, tightens key validation for ML-KEM, ML-DSA, FrodoKEM, Classic McEliece and XMSS so invalid keys fail consistently, adds an AVX2 path for ML-KEM, and warns in its documentation that Classic McEliece should only be used in a hybrid, following BSI.
- [`google/conscrypt`](https://github.com/google/conscrypt) — Google's Java security provider over BoringSSL, the TLS and JCE provider for Java applications on Android and OpenJDK. [2.7.0](https://github.com/google/conscrypt/releases/tag/2.7.0) (31 August 2026) makes X25519MLKEM768 the default for TLS and adds composite ML-DSA signatures (with Ed25519, ECDSA, RSA PKCS#1 v1.5 and RSA-PSS) and HashSLH-DSA with SHA-384, so a Java client that bundles Conscrypt gets hybrid key exchange from a dependency upgrade. [2.9.0](https://github.com/google/conscrypt/releases/tag/2.9.0) (4 November 2026) exposes the negotiated TLS group name through `Conscrypt.getGroupName(SSLSocket)` and `Conscrypt.getGroupName(SSLEngine)`, so an application can check, per connection, which group the handshake actually used. [2.10.0](https://github.com/google/conscrypt/releases/tag/2.10.0) (23 November 2026) adds `EXTERNAL-MU-ML-DSA` `Signature` algorithms for ML-DSA-44, -65 and -87 with a `SHAKE256-512` `MessageDigest`, so the message digest mu can be computed apart from the private key, and registers `ML-KEM-768` and `ML-KEM-1024` OID aliases for `KeyPairGenerator`, `KeyFactory` and `KEM`; it moves to BoringSSL 0.20261105.0.
- [GnuTLS](https://gitlab.com/gnutls/gnutls) — the GNU TLS library, post-quantum through [leancrypto](https://github.com/smuellerDD/leancrypto). Its [NEWS](https://gitlab.com/gnutls/gnutls/-/raw/master/NEWS) records the path: 3.8.8 (5 November 2024) adds experimental X25519MLKEM768 and SecP256r1MLKEM768 key exchange in TLS 1.3; 3.8.9 (7 February 2025) adds experimental ML-DSA, and SecP384r1MLKEM1024 key exchange; 3.8.10 (8 July 2025) drops liboqs, leaving leancrypto as the only PQC backend, and lets ML-DSA sign in TLS; and 3.8.14 (18 November 2026) adds `gnutls_pubkey_import_mldsa_raw()` and `gnutls_pubkey_export_mldsa_raw()` for the raw FIPS 204 public-key octet string, the form protocols outside X.509 carry.
- [libgcrypt](https://github.com/gpg/libgcrypt) — the GnuPG project's cryptographic library, and so the layer under GnuPG. Its [NEWS](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/gpg/libgcrypt/master/NEWS) records the path: 1.11.0 (19 June 2024) adds an API for key-encapsulation mechanisms with Kyber per the FIPS 203 initial public draft, Streamlined NTRU Prime sntrup761 and Classic McEliece; 1.11.1 moves Kyber to final FIPS 203; 1.12.0 (29 January 2026) adds Dilithium (ML-DSA) support; and 1.12.3 (26 August 2026) validates all KEM input lengths and lets `gcry_pk_genkey` accept and return a Kyber seed.
- [`jedisct1/libsodium`](https://github.com/jedisct1/libsodium) — the misuse-resistant library that many languages bind to. [1.0.22](https://github.com/jedisct1/libsodium/releases/tag/1.0.22-RELEASE) (9 April 2026) adds post-quantum key encapsulation: ML-KEM768 through the `crypto_kem_mlkem768_*()` functions, and X-Wing, ML-KEM768 combined with X25519, through the generic `crypto_kem_*()` functions, which the release calls the recommended KEM for most applications. X25519MLKEM768 has been supported since 0.23.22 (January 2025) and preferred since 0.23.27 (May 2025); 0.23.37 (February 2026) adds ML-KEM-1024 key exchange; and [0.23.44](https://github.com/rustls/rustls/releases/tag/v%2F0.23.44) (September 2026) enables ML-DSA certificates by default. Those certificates work only with private certificate hierarchies, because the public web PKI does not issue them. The security release [0.23.46](https://github.com/rustls/rustls/releases/tag/v%2F0.23.46) (14 October 2026) fixes a downgrade, [GHSA-w44r-93j7-qv28](https://github.com/rustls/rustls/security/advisories/GHSA-w44r-93j7-qv28) (rated low): the client recorded the server's chosen key-exchange group per server name before the server was authenticated, so one forged ServerHello selecting X25519 made later ClientHellos for that name carry only an X25519 share, and a server that accepted it completed a classical-only handshake with no error. Any client that caches key-share hints has the same problem to avoid.
- [`cloudflare/circl`](https://github.com/cloudflare/circl) — Go PQC and hybrid primitives, used in production at Cloudflare. The X-Wing hybrid KEM arrived in [v1.6.0](https://github.com/cloudflare/circl/releases/tag/v1.6.0) (February 2025) and SLH-DSA in [v1.6.2](https://github.com/cloudflare/circl/releases/tag/v1.6.2) (December 2025). [v1.6.5](https://github.com/cloudflare/circl/releases/tag/v1.6.5) (5 August 2026) is a hardening release: its Dilithium code rejects signatures with trailing data, eddilithium fails verification on a wrong-length signature, SLH-DSA no longer panics on an out-of-range prehash, and HPKE and KEM key unmarshaling require exact lengths.
- [Go standard library](https://go.dev/doc/go1.27) — `crypto/mlkem` since Go 1.24; Go 1.27 (August 2026) adds `crypto/mldsa`, ML-DSA in `crypto/x509` and TLS 1.3, and the MLKEM1024 key exchange. Post-quantum Go without a third-party dependency. [Go 1.27.4](https://go.dev/doc/devel/release#go1.27.4) (10 November 2026) takes ML-DSA back out of the TLS `signature_algorithms_cert` extension while keeping it in `signature_algorithms` ([issue #82536](https://github.com/golang/go/issues/82536)): some non-compliant middleboxes rejected ML-DSA there, under a rule aimed at an old OpenSSL denial-of-service bug, and connections failed widely. A warning for anyone adding new code points to a ClientHello.

## Protocol integration and deployment

- [Signal PQXDH](https://signal.org/blog/pqxdh/) — X25519 + ML-KEM-1024 for initial key agreement; the first large-scale PQC messaging deployment. The [Sparse Post-Quantum Ratchet](https://signal.org/blog/spqr/) extends post-quantum protection from that handshake into the ratchet itself, forming Signal's Triple Ratchet.
- [JEP 527](https://openjdk.org/jeps/527) — JDK 27 (September 2026) puts X25519MLKEM768 first in the default TLS 1.3 groups, so any Java TLS client left on the `javax.net.ssl` defaults offers hybrid key exchange without a code change. The October 2026 OpenJDK updates carry it back to the long-term-support lines: [25.0.5](https://github.com/openjdk/jdk25u-dev/commit/83e90e7d76e9ca804384d47a4d529e91f79d8981) (tagged 20 October) backports JEP 527 with X25519MLKEM768 first in the default groups, and [21.0.13](https://github.com/openjdk/jdk21u-dev/commit/6de158a4cc3f099956af964392b0125bb5769376) (tagged the same day) backports only the ML-KEM and ML-DSA providers of JEPs 496 and 497, so JDK 21 gets the algorithms but keeps classical TLS defaults. In mainline, the [JDK-8385968 commit](https://github.com/openjdk/jdk/commit/6fc8c86b80fc23b1497a777bd6a4a4f21028c97d) of 18 November 2026 lets SunJSSE use a TLS 1.3 hybrid group that a third-party security provider implements as JCA `KeyPairGenerator` and `KEM` services, and moves the JDK's own X25519MLKEM768, SecP256r1MLKEM768 and SecP384r1MLKEM1024 into SunJCE.
- [Python 3.15 `ssl`](https://docs.python.org/3.15/whatsnew/3.15.html) — released 9 October 2026, the standard library's TLS module gains control over key agreement: `SSLContext.set_groups()` takes an OpenSSL group list where `set_ecdh_curve()` took one curve, and by the release notes supports finite-field and post-quantum groups and controls which key shares the handshake sends; `SSLSocket.group()` returns the group a connection negotiated (OpenSSL 3.2 or later) and `SSLContext.get_groups()` lists the groups available (OpenSSL 3.5 or later). With `set_client_sigalgs()`, `set_server_sigalgs()`, `client_sigalg()` and `server_sigalg()` for signature algorithms, a Python program can require a post-quantum group and check, per connection, what the handshake used, without a third-party TLS binding.
- [Erlang/OTP 29](https://www.erlang.org/news/188) — released 13 May 2026, it makes post-quantum the default in both of its protocol stacks. In `ssl`, the hybrid x25519mlkem768 is the most preferred key exchange group in the default configuration, and the most preferred signature algorithms are ML-DSA followed by SLH-DSA (`slh_dsa_sha2_256f`), which apply where such a certificate is configured; in `ssh`, the default key exchange is `mlkem768x25519-sha256`, with the other algorithms used when a peer does not support it. The [`ssl` release notes](https://www.erlang.org/doc/apps/ssl/notes.html) say all of these were available in OTP 28.4 without being preferred, and that secp384r1mlkem1024, secp256r1mlkem768 and the plain ML-KEM groups have to be configured. So an Erlang or Elixir service on the defaults gets hybrid key exchange by upgrading OTP.
- [OpenSSH](https://www.openssh.com/releasenotes.html) — `mlkem768x25519-sha256` is the default key exchange from OpenSSH 10.0; `sntrup761x25519-sha512` has shipped since 9.0. Authentication followed in 10.4 (July 2026) with experimental composite ML-DSA-44 + Ed25519 keys (`ssh-keygen -t mldsa44-ed25519`); 10.6 (October 2026) enables `ssh-mldsa44-ed25519` as a regular signature algorithm without the experimental `@openssh.com` suffix, so keys made with 10.4 or 10.5 must be regenerated, and adds a server-side `WarnWeakCrypto` option, on by default, that logs any client whose key agreement is not post-quantum safe; 10.6p2 (November 2026) also accepts it as a certificate-authority signature algorithm, so SSH CAs can sign host and user certificates with it.
- [`open-quantum-safe/openssh`](https://github.com/open-quantum-safe/openssh) — experimental OQS-enabled OpenSSH for algorithms not upstream.
- [GnuPG](https://gnupg.org/) — post-quantum OpenPGP encryption in two formats, recorded in its [NEWS](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/gpg/gnupg/master/NEWS). 2.5.0 (5 July 2024) adds composite Kyber+ECC public-key algorithms as experimental, and 2.5.1 (12 September 2024) moves them to the final FIPS 203 and LibrePGP specifications, dropping the 2.5.0 keys. 2.5.23 (22 September 2026) adds the `--allow-9980` option, which enables processing of RFC 9980 data structures and, in that mode, encryption using ML-KEM with X448, X25519, Brainpool or NIST curves, the format Bouncy Castle 1.87 implements. The NEWS recommends the option only where external policy demands RFC 9980 and points everyone else to the LibrePGP Kyber algorithm, so a deployment has to know which of the two its correspondents read. 2.5.24 (23 September 2026) fixes a regression in 2.5.23.
- [`strongswan/strongswan`](https://github.com/strongswan/strongswan) — the open-source IKEv2 and IPsec implementation, and the one ANSSI's post-quantum IPsec note ([ANSSI-FT-117](https://messervices.cyber.gouv.fr/documents-guides/transition_post_quantique_ipsec.pdf), February 2026) cites for RFC 8784 PPK and RFC 9370 hybrid key exchange. [6.0.0](https://github.com/strongswan/strongswan/releases/tag/6.0.0) (3 December 2024) adds RFC 9370's multiple IKEv2 key exchanges, configured in a proposal with `ke1_` to `ke7_` prefixes such as `ke1_mlkem768`, and ML-KEM-512, -768 and -1024 through Botan 3.6.0 or later, wolfSSL 5.7.4 or later, AWS-LC 1.37.0 or later and its own new `ml` plugin; it removes the deprecated `bliss`, `newhope` and `ntru` plugins. [6.0.2](https://github.com/strongswan/strongswan/releases/tag/6.0.2) (14 July 2025) supports ML-KEM through OpenSSL 3.5 or later, and [6.1.0](https://github.com/strongswan/strongswan/releases/tag/6.1.0) (7 September 2026) makes every ML-KEM implementation refuse to return an empty shared secret and its `ml` plugin [rejects non-compliant ML-KEM public keys](https://github.com/strongswan/strongswan/commit/4f3e572b5bf2df169fa46fb040dc8ab0a0e19e4a).
- [Linux kernel: ML-DSA module signatures](https://docs.kernel.org/admin-guide/module-signing.html) — the kernel's module-signing facility supports RSA, NIST P-384 ECDSA and FIPS 204 ML-DSA. Linux 7.0 (tagged 12 April 2026) carries the pieces: [`CRYPTO_MLDSA`](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/v7.0/crypto/Kconfig), which implements signature verification only, and a [PKCS#7 option](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/v7.0/crypto/asymmetric_keys/Kconfig) that lets ML-DSA module signatures carry authenticated attributes, because OpenSSL before 4.0 cannot produce them without. [`certs/Kconfig`](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/master/certs/Kconfig) offers ML-DSA-44, -65 and -87 as module signing key types, which need OpenSSL 3.5 or later on the build machine. A boot chain that verifies its kernel modules can move that signature to a post-quantum algorithm with a build option.
- [`aws/s2n-tls`](https://github.com/aws/s2n-tls) — TLS implementation with hybrid PQC key exchange. [v1.7.9](https://github.com/aws/s2n-tls/releases/tag/v1.7.9) (3 September 2026) adds hybrid ML-KEM counterparts of three older security policies, `20200207_pq`, `20230317_pq` and `CloudFront-TLS-1-2-2021-PQ`, so services pinned to those policies have a post-quantum path. In [v1.7.14](https://github.com/aws/s2n-tls/releases/tag/v1.7.14) (13 November 2026), a client that sends an X25519MLKEM768 share and prefers X25519 reuses the hybrid's X25519 key for its classical share, saving one key generation per ClientHello.
- [AWS KMS: ML-KEM keys](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/kms/latest/developerguide/pqkeys.html) — since 26 October 2026 ([announcement](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2026/10/kms-ml-kem-post-quantum/)) KMS creates ML-KEM-512, -768 and -1024 key pairs (`ML_KEM_512`, `ML_KEM_768`, `ML_KEM_1024`, key usage `ENCAPSULATE_DECAPSULATE`) whose private keys stay in FIPS 140-3 Level 3 HSMs. `Encapsulate` returns a random 32-byte shared secret with its ciphertext, `Decapsulate` recovers it, and `GetPublicKey` lets a client encapsulate locally with AWS-LC, OpenSSL or Bouncy Castle. - [Google Cloud KMS: post-quantum signing](https://docs.cloud.google.com/kms/docs/release-notes) — generally available since 16 July 2026: ML-DSA-44, -65 and -87, each also with external mu, and SLH-DSA-SHA2-128s, pure and prehashed with SHA-256. Since 6 August 2026 a preview of key import can wrap the imported key with HPKE over X-Wing, ML-KEM-768 or ML-KEM-1024.
- [Akamai: end-to-end post-quantum cryptography](https://www.akamai.com/blog/security/2026/aug/future-proofing-internet-akamai-achieves-end-to-end-pqc) — generally available on the Enhanced TLS network since 12 August 2026: hybrid X25519MLKEM768 is on by default from client to Akamai, with an opt-out in Property Manager, and between Akamai tiers, and opt-in from Akamai to origin since June 2026, so every hop of a request can use hybrid key exchange once the origin opts in.
- [Apple: prepare your network for quantum-secure encryption in TLS](https://support.apple.com/en-us/122756) — iOS, iPadOS, macOS, tvOS, visionOS and watchOS 26 offer X25519MLKEM768 by default from URLSession and the Network framework, so every app on those APIs now sends a hybrid key share; the [platform security guide](https://support.apple.com/guide/security/quantum-secure-cryptography-apple-devices-secc7c82e533/web) covers CryptoKit's ML-KEM and the rest. Written for the people whose middleboxes will see the larger ClientHello. On 22 May 2026 Apple [published](https://security.apple.com/blog/formal-verification-corecrypto) the [corecrypto](https://github.com/apple/corecrypto) ML-KEM and ML-DSA code that CryptoKit relies on, with more than 50,000 proof steps in Isabelle, SAW and Cryptol showing that the C matches FIPS 203 and 204 and that the ARM64 assembly matches the C. The proofs found a missing step in an early ML-DSA implementation that could silently produce wrong output, and they assume a correct compiler. The source is under an evaluation-only licence, for research and verification rather than reuse.
- [Windows TLS: preparing for a post-quantum future](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/microsoft-security-blog/preparing-for-a-post-quantum-future-with-tls-in-windows/4572619) — Microsoft, 17 November 2026. Hybrid key exchange reached the Windows TLS stack earlier in 2026, and the October 2026 security update adds pure ML-KEM key exchange and ML-DSA authentication on Windows 11 24H2 and later and Windows Server 2025; for Server 2025 that is [KB5126646](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/servicing/os/windows-server/2026/10/kb5126646-windows-server-2025-security-update) (13 October 2026), which adds ML-KEM key exchange in TLS 1.3. - [Modern Algorithms in the Web Cryptography API](https://wicg.github.io/webcrypto-modern-algos/) — a WICG draft (23 October 2026) adding ML-KEM-512, -768 and -1024, three hybrid KEMs (MLKEM768-P256, MLKEM768-X25519 and MLKEM1024-P384), ML-DSA and SLH-DSA to the browser's built-in `crypto.subtle`, alongside ChaCha20-Poly1305, AES-OCB, the SHA-3 family and Argon2, so web pages get post-quantum primitives from the browser instead of bundling their own. [Cloudflare Workers](https://blog.cloudflare.com/workers-ml-kem-ml-dsa-support/) has offered ML-KEM-768 and -1024 and ML-DSA-44, -65 and -87 under the same API since 1 October 2026, behind the `webcrypto_modern_algorithms` compatibility flag, with ML-KEM-512 left out because its BoringSSL does not expose it.
- [Cloudflare: Automatic Key Exchange](https://blog.cloudflare.com/automatic-key-exchange-for-origins/) — September 2026: Cloudflare probes each customer origin for the key agreement it supports and leads with X25519MLKEM768 wherever the origin speaks it, which moved the origin side of some 45 billion daily connections to post-quantum key exchange without customer action.
- [Cloudflare: post-quantum DNSSEC on 1.1.1.1](https://blog.cloudflare.com/post-quantum-dnssec-1111/) — September 2026: the resolver validates ML-DSA-44 signatures (2,420 bytes against ECDSA's 64) and uses the parent's DS records to refuse a downgrade to a classical signature where the zone advertises ML-DSA.
- [Cloudflare: post-quantum geographically fenced key management](https://blog.cloudflare.com/post-quantum-geo-key-manager/) — 23 October 2026, a closed beta for the Data Localization Suite, with general availability planned for the first quarter of 2027: private keys that only servers in a chosen region can unwrap, now wrapped with HPKE (RFC 9180) using a hybrid ML-KEM-768 and X25519 KEM, HKDF-SHA-256 and AES-256-GCM. The 2022 design used attribute-based encryption, which Cloudflare dropped for lack of a production-ready post-quantum ABE scheme; each region instead has a 256-bit root key from which per-service HPKE key pairs are derived. A worked example of replacing an advanced primitive that has no post-quantum equivalent with a simpler key hierarchy.
- [Cloudflare PQC reports](https://blog.cloudflare.com/pq-2025/) — the best public measurements of real-world PQC adoption; see also [the 2024 edition](https://blog.cloudflare.com/pq-2024/) and the per-domain [post-quantum visibility](https://blog.cloudflare.com/post-quantum-visibility/) added in September 2026. Radar's [origin graph](https://blog.cloudflare.com/radar-origin-pq-key-transparency-aspa/) (27 February 2026) tracks the share of customer origins supporting X25519MLKEM768: about 10% could then benefit from post-quantum-preferred key agreement, against under 1% at the start of 2025.
- [Cloudflare Research: post-quantum anonymous credentials for Privacy Pass](https://blog.cloudflare.com/vole-mole/) — Bas Westerbaan, 6 November 2026, with code at [`cloudflareresearch/vole-mole`](https://github.com/cloudflareresearch/vole-mole) (C++, BSD-3-Clause, experimental and unaudited by its own README). Privacy Pass tokens rest on RSA blind signatures, so a quantum attacker could forge them, though not link a token to its issuance. Three constructions build on UOV signatures (135-byte signatures, 46.6 KB public keys) proved in VOLE-in-the-head at FAEST-EM-128s parameters: PoMFRIT, a blind signature, at under 1 KB to issue and 4.3 KB to redeem; VOLE-IHAT, a token that also hides its issuer, at 5.6 KB and 6.5 KB; and VOLE-ACT, an anonymous credit token whose balance the issuer updates without learning it, at 5.4 KB and 12.0 KB. Every step takes under 2 ms on an Apple M4 Pro except VOLE-IHAT redemption, about 7 ms with 64 anchors. PoMFRIT's optimisations rest on a new one-more-UOV assumption.
- [Secria VPN whitepaper](https://secria.me/whitepaper/vpn/) — a WireGuard deployment that gets post-quantum key exchange without patching WireGuard: each connection gets a fresh preshared key sealed to an ML-KEM-1024 encapsulation key and delivered over X25519 + ML-KEM-768 hybrid TLS, then folded into the stock Noise IKpsk2 handshake. Because the PSK is one-shot and client-initiated rather than periodically rotated, it avoids the simultaneous-initiation race that symmetric rotation schemes have to handle.

Everything above is key exchange, which defends against harvest-now-decrypt-later.
Authentication is the harder half: a post-quantum certificate chain in every
handshake costs kilobytes.

- [Chromium post-quantum authentication roadmap](https://www.chromium.org/Home/chromium-security/post-quantum-auth-roadmap/) — the browser plan for PQ authentication in TLS: PQ-secure CAs issuing Merkle Tree Certificates, alongside ML-DSA TLS keys.
- [Google: cultivating a robust and efficient quantum-safe HTTPS](https://blog.google/security/cultivating-a-robust-and-efficient-quantum-safe-https/) — February 2026, the announcement behind that roadmap: Chrome will not add post-quantum X.509 chains to its root store but is building a Chrome Quantum-resistant Root Store for Merkle Tree Certificates, in three phases through Q3 2027, starting with a feasibility study with Cloudflare.
- [Chrome Quantum-resistant Root Program policy](https://googlechrome.github.io/chromerootprogram/cqrp/draft-policy/) — the rules a Merkle Tree Certificate CA has to meet for Chrome, in draft: version 0.4.0 (28 October 2026), designated release candidate 1.0.0 and aligned with draft-07 of the MTC specification. Cosigner keys must be ML-DSA-44; a standalone certificate needs at least two cosignatures, one from the CA and one from a recognised mirror run by an unaffiliated operator; subscriber certificates last at most 7 days under a CA's first active cosigner key and 47 days under the others; and initial eligibility is limited to organisations that ran a usable Certificate Transparency log before 1 February 2026. The [FAQ](https://googlechrome.github.io/chromerootprogram/cqrp/faq/) states that Chrome supports ML-DSA in private PKI hierarchies from Chrome 150, so post-quantum X.509 chains work inside an enterprise while the public store takes only MTCs, and dates the phases: public MTC bootstrapping in Q1 2027 and the root store in Q3 2027. The [testing instructions](https://googlechrome.github.io/chromerootprogram/cqrp/testing/) cover the test root store in Chrome 152 or later, behind the `verify-mtcs` and `test-root-store` flags. Version 5.0.0 of that [store](https://googlechrome.github.io/chromerootprogram/cqrp/root-store) ([JSON](https://www.gstatic.com/mtcs/cosigners/v1/cosigners.json), 17 November 2026) names five issuing CAs, TrustAsia Test CA 3 and 4, DigiCert Test CA 1, mtcs.dev Test CA 2 and Sectigo Test CA 1, and mirrors run by TrustAsia, Geomys, DigiCert, mtcs.dev and Sectigo, all for untrusted validation only and on draft-07. - [Let's Encrypt: the path to post-quantum certificates](https://letsencrypt.org/2026/06/03/pq-certs.html) — Let's Encrypt choosing Merkle Tree Certificates (June 2026), with the argument that transparency becomes a property of issuance rather than a log bolted on afterwards. On 5 November 2026 the Sovereign Tech Agency [contracted more than €1M](https://letsencrypt.org/2026/11/05/sta-pq-funding) for the Merkle Tree Certificate authority, running through the end of 2027 and covering standardisation at the IETF and C2SP, root-program work, and the move from staging tests to a production launch; the largest single investment in post-quantum readiness at Let's Encrypt to date, by its own account.
- [Cloudflare: building a certificate authority for the whole Internet](https://blog.cloudflare.com/cloudflare-certificate-authority/) — September 2026: Cloudflare becomes a public CA, with a definitive agreement to acquire a broadly trusted root from GlobalSign and applications to the Chrome, Apple, Microsoft and Mozilla root programs. ACME-first, issuing only to clients that support ACME Renewal Information ([RFC 9773](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9773.html)), with the first Merkle Tree Certificates in the first quarter of 2027. The second CA, after Let's Encrypt, to commit to MTCs in writing. The companion post, [building a post-quantum CA with MTCs](https://blog.cloudflare.com/pq-ca-with-mtcs/), describes the CA itself: an ACME server forked from Boulder, mirroring cosigners in its Rust transparency log Azul, and standard MTC issuance at no cost. The experiment used classical signatures and ended in August 2026.
- [IAB workshop on post-quantum authentication: lessons from Prague](https://www.ietf.org/blog/iab-pq-workshop-impressions/) — 12 November 2026, by IAB members Yaroslav Rosomakho and Nick Sullivan: first impressions from the two-day workshop the Internet Architecture Board held in Prague on 11 and 12 October 2026, with 22 position papers. Nobody reported a security failure in ML-DSA or SLH-DSA; the four obstacles that kept coming up are deployment ones: new keys and signatures break old size limits, verifiers and their roots have the longest deployment timeline but must move first, several proposals do more than swap a classical signature algorithm for a post-quantum one, and regulatory requirements and technical constraints are driving different approaches. It carries measurements: in a Cisco SD-WAN simulation at 3% packet loss, 16.3% of ML-DSA-87 handshakes took more than five seconds over DTLS 1.2 against 0.2% over DTLS 1.3, and a UK GDS identity flow grew from 6.7 KB to 44.5 KB with ML-DSA-65 signatures and ML-KEM-768 key exchange.

## Blockchain and consensus

Chains hit PQC problems the web does not, and — importantly — not the *same*
problem as each other. Bitcoin's is public-key exposure on an immutable ledger
under rough-consensus governance; Ethereum's is signature size at validator
scale, which is why its flagship artifact is a zkVM rather than a signature
library; Cosmos's is key-type negotiation across interoperable chains. Progress
inverts exposure: the chain with the sharpest exposure has two Draft BIPs, and
the least-discussed of the three has a PQ signature scheme in released code.

**Ethereum.** Hash-based signatures rest on the most conservative assumption
available, but at a kilobyte or more each they cannot go on chain one per
validator, so most of the work is *aggregation*.

- [Post-Quantum Ethereum](https://pq.ethereum.org/) — the hub for Ethereum's post-quantum effort, and the fastest way to see current status.
- [Ethereum quantum-resistance roadmap](https://ethereum.org/roadmap/security/quantum-resistance/) — why the consensus layer is changing, in protocol terms.
- [EIP-8355, Precompiles for ML-DSA Verification](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-8355) — ML-DSA verification for contracts at the execution layer: a Draft EIP, merged September 2026. It was proposed for the Hegotá upgrade, and on 8 October 2026, with the decisions of All Core Devs Execution call 247, the [Hegotá meta EIP](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-8081) moved it to Declined for Inclusion. Test vectors merged on 7 November 2026 ([PR #12610](https://github.com/ethereum/EIPs/pull/12610)) grow from three ML-DSA-44 cases to 60 Wycheproof-derived cases across ML-DSA-44, -65 and -87, with reject cases for out-of-range coefficients, non-canonical hints, norm-bound failures and context-bound signatures, since the precompile uses the empty context.
- [EIP-8141, Frame Transaction](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-8141) — the account change that Hegotá does schedule: a Draft EIP (created 29 January 2026), listed as Scheduled for Inclusion in the same meta EIP. A frame transaction is a sequence of contract calls that validate it, approve gas and execute, so an account is no longer tied to its ECDSA key and can rotate keys natively; the EIP's motivation calls this a native off-ramp from elliptic-curve authentication to post-quantum systems. It defines SECP256K1 and P256 signature types and an ARBITRARY type for bespoke witnesses, and names no post-quantum scheme itself.
- [EIP-8288, In-mempool signature and proof aggregation](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-8288) — the aggregation design brought to execution-layer transactions: a transaction declares leanSPHINCS signatures and leanSTARK proofs as dependencies instead of verifying them in the EVM, mempool nodes merge them, and each block carries one recursive STARK covering all of them. A Draft EIP (created June 2026); the leanSTARK scheme stays reserved until its hash is fixed, BLAKE2s in current tooling with BLAKE3 the leading replacement.
- [EIP-8450, Post-Quantum Consensus Key Registry](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-8450) — a Draft EIP (created 15 October 2026) that prepares the consensus layer for replacing BLS: the beacon state gains lists of post-quantum key material, a leanXMSS public key and a hash-based VRF tree root, 32 bytes each, for every validator, and a leanXMSS key with a zero VRF root for every builder, plus lists recording the epoch of each one's last key change. A registration is a new beacon block operation, at most 8 per block, carrying a leanXMSS proof of possession (signatures are 2,404 bytes) and authorised by the registrant's existing BLS key. Registration changes no duties: the EIP leaves moving them to these keys to a later fork.
- [EIP-8425, Quantum Freeze and Account Recovery](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-8425) — a Draft EIP (created 27 September 2026): from a global freeze timestamp, transactions authenticated by a secp256k1 ECDSA sender signature are invalid, EIP-7702 ECDSA authorisations can no longer change delegation, and the `ecRecover` precompile is removed. Owners install successor authorisation along one of two paths: a recovery commitment registered before a cutoff, or a proof of knowledge of the original seed that cannot be derived from the ECDSA key.
- [`leanEthereum/leanVM`](https://github.com/leanEthereum/leanVM) — "minimal hash-based zkVM, for post-quantum Ethereum", built for recursive aggregation of post-quantum signatures. This is the piece that makes hash-based signatures affordable at validator scale. v0.10 (September 2026) rewrote it over binary fields with BLAKE2s, which the README calls a placeholder hash, and specifies its own XMSS with 32-byte public keys and 1,208-byte signatures, at about 128-bit classical and 64-bit quantum (QROM) security. Its client bindings moved into the repository with [bindings-v0.12.0](https://github.com/leanEthereum/leanVM/releases/tag/bindings-v0.12.0) (19 November 2026; Go module `leanvm.org/go`, and aggregates from 0.11.x and 0.12.0 reject each other), and [bindings-v0.13.0](https://github.com/leanEthereum/leanVM/releases/tag/bindings-v0.13.0) the same day replaces in-process aggregation with `Sidecar.aggregate` and `Sidecar.verify` calls to a running `leanvm-sidecar` process, Unix only. Four more followed on 20 November 2026: 0.13.1, then 0.14.0 loads leanXMSS keys from a tree cache, [bindings-v0.15.0](https://github.com/leanEthereum/leanVM/releases/tag/bindings-v0.15.0), a breaking change, has leanXMSS keys store 2^8 nodes per tree and cache 4 bottom trees, at most 20 KiB of cache per key, and [bindings-v0.16.0](https://github.com/leanEthereum/leanVM/releases/tag/bindings-v0.16.0) adds a VRF tree with pruning and a cached middle layer.
- [`leanEthereum/leanSig`](https://github.com/leanEthereum/leanSig) — Rust prototype of the Poseidon-based generalized XMSS family, built on tweakable hash functions and incomparable encodings. It grew out of the research implementation for [eprint 2025/055](https://eprint.iacr.org/2025/055), which is no longer maintained. Explicitly unaudited and not for production.
- [`ProjectZKM/OPSuccinct`](https://github.com/ProjectZKM/OPSuccinct) — a post-quantum take on the zk OP-stack validity bridge: a Solidity verifier that checks Ziren's compact shrink proofs (WHIR over KoalaBear, Keccak256 transcript) directly on an EVM chain, where OP Succinct-style bridges normally wrap the proof in pairing-based BN254 Groth16 or PLONK. Dropping the wrap keeps the proof's soundness on hash assumptions alone, and the README measures what that costs: about 13.7M gas to verify a 182 KB proof, inside Osaka's 16.8M per-transaction cap. Its own bridge document is explicit about scope: blob KZG, account signatures, consensus and governance stay classical, and the Metis guest and contract upgrades it targets are proposals.

**Bitcoin.** Two Draft BIPs. BIP-360 removes Taproot's key-path spend so no
bare public key is exposed; it is an output type, not a signature scheme.
BIP-361 formally `Requires: TBD Post Quantum Signature BIP`. A first candidate,
SHRINCS, was posted to bitcoin-dev in August 2026, and a third party has since
machine-checked its leaf one-time signature.

- [BIP-360, Pay-to-Merkle-Root (P2MR)](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0360.mediawiki) — soft-fork output type defending against long-exposure attacks. Explicitly not short-exposure, and explicitly not a signature scheme.
- [BIP-361, Post Quantum Migration and Legacy Signature Sunset](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0361.mediawiki) — phased sunset of legacy ECDSA/Schnorr. States that as of 1 March 2026 over 34% of all bitcoin have revealed a public key on chain.
- [Bitcoin Optech: quantum resistance](https://bitcoinops.org/en/topics/quantum-resistance/) — the least breathless running summary of where the debate stands.
- [`BlockstreamResearch/shrincs-cpp`](https://github.com/BlockstreamResearch/shrincs-cpp) — a C++ implementation whose `main` branch predates the draft: `w = 256` over 16 chains against the draft's `w = 16` over 32, and PORS+FP where the draft settled on FORS to stay black-box compatible with FIPS 205. The [`feature/shrincs_bip`](https://github.com/BlockstreamResearch/shrincs-cpp/tree/feature/shrincs_bip) branch follows the draft's structure (`w = 16` over 32 chains, FORS), with a benchmark and KAT harness.
- [`SHRINCS/shrincs-bip`](https://github.com/SHRINCS/shrincs-bip) — the draft itself, and the first concrete answer to BIP-361's missing dependency. Semi-stateful: a 548-byte stateful signature backed by an SLH-DSA fallback inside every key pair, so losing state costs signature size rather than funds. September 2026 gave every ADRS type its own address type and bound message domain separation in one place; a deterministic test-vector generator was merged on 26 October 2026 ([PR #72](https://github.com/SHRINCS/shrincs-bip/pull/72)), committing only SHA-256 hashes of the vectors and leaving out invalid-signature cases and longer messages, and October's refactors are explicitly aimed at making a correctness proof easier. A [commit of 23 November 2026](https://github.com/SHRINCS/shrincs-bip/commit/4e831c75794dbe6bd7466e077c02754d025b56b9) closes a state-reuse path in the specification: where incrementing the last usable counter would overflow, a signer must record exhaustion in persistent storage before signing with that counter.
- [`remix7531/libshrincs`](https://github.com/remix7531/libshrincs) — the first machine-checked piece of SHRINCS: WOTS+C, the leaf one-time signature, transcribed from the shrincs-bip Python reference and tested against the same reference vectors. VST (Rocq 9.2 with CompCert) proves that the C, its own SHA-256 included, computes the specification, and SSProve bounds a forger's advantage in a known-message experiment by six explicit reductions to hash games on truncated SHA-256; the project declares no assumption of its own beyond what its proof libraries bring. The theorem covers WOTS+C, not the full FXMSS construction. MIT.

**Cosmos.** A FIPS-standardized scheme in released consensus code, rather than
in a proposal.

- [Cosmos SDK `UPGRADING.md`](https://github.com/cosmos/cosmos-sdk/blob/main/UPGRADING.md) — v0.55 ([released 2026-07-28](https://github.com/cosmos/cosmos-sdk/releases/tag/v0.55.0)) registers ML-DSA-65 (FIPS 204) as a validator consensus key type ([PR #26436](https://github.com/cosmos/cosmos-sdk/pull/26436)), opt-in through the consensus parameter `validator.pub_key_types`, and as an account key type ([PR #26472](https://github.com/cosmos/cosmos-sdk/pull/26472)). Read the IBC warning: light clients verify commit signatures with the *counterparty's* compiled-in crypto, so enabling a new key type before counterparties can verify it stops packet flow and eventually expires the client. The counterparty needs only the verification code, which [CometBFT v0.38.26](https://github.com/cometbft/cometbft/releases/tag/v0.38.26) backports for chains that stay on v0.38; [CometBFT v0.40.0](https://github.com/cometbft/cometbft/releases/tag/v0.40.0) (27 July 2026) adds `mldsa65` as a validator key type.
- [Post-quantum keys](https://docs.cosmos.network/sdk/latest/keys/post-quantum-keys) and [Migrate a validator to ML-DSA](https://docs.cosmos.network/sdk/latest/keys/migrate-validator-ml-dsa) — the operator guides.

**Beyond these three.** Algorand, NEAR and QRL run post-quantum account signatures on
mainnet. Algorand's scheme is Falcon, which NIST selected but has not yet
standardized: with FIPS 206 unpublished, this is Falcon rather than FN-DSA.
NEAR's is ML-DSA-65, already a published standard (FIPS 204). QRL's is XMSS
(RFC 8391), stateful and hash-based. Zcash took a different route: rather than
change signatures, it added shielded notes that a recovery protocol could
reclaim after a quantum break.

- [`algorand/go-algorand` v5.0.0](https://github.com/algorand/go-algorand/releases/tag/v5.0.0-stable) — the consensus upgrade (August 2026) adding native Falcon-1024 account signatures, post-quantum delegated LogicSigs, and `algokey pq` key management. [v5.1.0-beta](https://github.com/algorand/go-algorand/releases/tag/v5.1.0-beta) (6 November 2026) carries Falcon-512 beside Falcon-1024, an `mldsa_verify` AVM opcode, a post-quantum LogicSig account scheme and hybrid accounts, all shipped disabled. [v5.1.1-stable](https://github.com/algorand/go-algorand/releases/tag/v5.1.1-stable) (19 November 2026), the first stable 5.1 release, ships Falcon-512, `mldsa_verify`, the `ls` post-quantum LogicSig scheme and Ed25519 hashed accounts under pqsig, all disabled, and drops the pre-v42 `PQSigEnabled` gate.
- [FractalAI](https://github.com/johnInarti/pqc-receipt-anchor) — a small Rust L1 that signs block consensus with ML-DSA-65 outright, where Cosmos registers it as an opt-in key type. Its interest is operational rather than architectural: a public key directory at [`/.well-known/x402-receipt-keys`](https://fractalai.net.co/.well-known/x402-receipt-keys) has been rotated through three epochs with each root committing to its predecessor, and still publishes the superseded key ids so older receipts keep verifying; the companion repository is a write-once Solidity anchor plus an offline verifier that checks the ML-DSA-65 signature locally and its on-chain timestamp against any RPC, so a receipt can be shown to predate a block without trusting the issuer. Read it as a worked example, not a security claim: one validator, no external audit, and no FIPS 140-3 validation of the module.
- [`MystenLabs/sui`](https://github.com/MystenLabs/sui) — ML-DSA-65 (FIPS 204) account signatures, standalone and as multisig members, behind the `mldsa65_auth` [protocol flag](https://github.com/MystenLabs/sui/blob/mainnet-v1.84.1/crates/sui-protocol-config/src/lib.rs), which protocol version 139 turns on for devnet. [mainnet-v1.84.1](https://github.com/MystenLabs/sui/releases/tag/mainnet-v1.84.1) (18 November 2026, protocol 142) carries the code, and its GraphQL signature verification accepts ML-DSA-65 multisig members whatever a network's activation rules.
- [`near/nearcore` 2.13.0](https://github.com/near/nearcore/releases/tag/2.13.0) — the protocol upgrade (voting from 2026-07-20) that stabilises ML-DSA-65 as a third transaction-signature and access-key scheme beside ed25519 and secp256k1 ([PR #15731](https://github.com/near/nearcore/pull/15731)). An account opts in by adding an ML-DSA-65 access key, so nothing has to move to a new address. The state trie stores a 32-byte SHA3-256 hash of the 1,952-byte public key rather than the key, and each ML-DSA-65 verification is charged an extra 100 Ggas. The scope stops at transactions: validator and staking keys, and block and chunk signatures, stay ed25519. [2.14.0](https://github.com/near/nearcore/releases/tag/2.14.0) (26 October 2026, protocol 86 to 88, voting from 4 and 6 November) adds an `ml_dsa_verify` host function so contracts can verify ML-DSA-65 signatures on chain, at 540 Ggas plus 11 Mgas per byte, over the message as-is with an empty context string, and fixes the gas-key storage fee to charge for the 32-byte key hash rather than the full key. The same release stabilises universal accounts, a `0u` account scheme that unifies implicit and contract-created account creation under what its notes call one post-quantum-safe derivation. Mainnet has since moved to protocol 88 ([RPC status](https://rpc.mainnet.near.org/status)), so contracts there can call `ml_dsa_verify`.
- [Qanum](https://qanum.org/) — a Rust proof-of-work UTXO layer 1 built around the size of post-quantum signatures rather than retrofitted: ML-DSA-87 and SLH-DSA-SHAKE-256s spends with no elliptic-curve path, authorization data kept apart from base block data under its own limit, a per-block verification budget, and a commitment to the coin set (`utxo_root`) in every header. Its [whitepaper](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23474681) states that the SHA3-256 proof of work keeps Grover's quadratic advantage like any other. Read it as a public testnet: no external audit, and no mainnet.
- [`stellar/stellar-core` v30.0.0](https://github.com/stellar/stellar-core/releases/tag/v30.0.0) — the release (16 October 2026) that bumps Stellar to protocol 30, which includes [CAP-0087](https://github.com/stellar/stellar-protocol/blob/master/core/cap-0087.md): three host functions, `verify_sig_ml_dsa_44`, `verify_sig_ml_dsa_65` and `verify_sig_ml_dsa_87`, with which a Soroban contract verifies pure FIPS 204 ML-DSA signatures, passing a context string of 0 to 255 bytes and trapping on a failed verification, at a cost linear in the combined message and context length. [Testnet](https://horizon-testnet.stellar.org/) runs protocol 30. The CAP is exact about scope: it does not change the network's transaction signature scheme, which is Ed25519, and "does not make Stellar post-quantum secure".
- [`theQRL/QRL`](https://github.com/theQRL/QRL) — the Quantum Resistant Ledger: a Python proof-of-work ledger that signs with XMSS instead of ECDSA, on a mainnet its [site](https://www.theqrl.org/) describes as built on XMSS, and which it calls the first industrial implementation of the IETF-specified scheme. The one chain here where stateful-signature key management is a wallet's everyday problem rather than a firmware signer's. MIT. Its Go client, [`theQRL/go-qrl`](https://github.com/theQRL/go-qrl), moved from Dilithium 5 to ML-DSA-87 in [v0.3.0](https://github.com/theQRL/go-qrl/releases/tag/v0.3.0) (2 April 2026), with a Testnet V2 configuration; [v0.3.1](https://github.com/theQRL/go-qrl/releases/tag/v0.3.1) (8 April 2026) fixes the `qrl_sign` API corrupting ML-DSA-87 signatures.
- [ZIP 2005, Ironwood Quantum Recoverability](https://zips.z.cash/zip-2005) — Zcash's hedge, active on mainnet since the NU6.3 upgrade at block 3,428,143 ([ZIP 258](https://zips.z.cash/zip-0258); [Zebra 6.0.0](https://github.com/ZcashFoundation/zebra/releases/tag/v6.0.0), July 2026). Sapling and Orchard note commitments are not post-quantum binding, so an adversary able to compute discrete logarithms could inflate the supply or steal funds. Recoverable notes fold every note field into the derivation of their commitment randomness, and wallets are told to move funds into them, so that a later shielded recovery protocol could return those funds after a quantum break. The ZIP keeps that recovery protocol out of consensus: this protects funds, not signatures.

**Proof systems.** Aggregating post-quantum signatures and verifying zkVM output
without pairings both need a post-quantum succinct proof. In 2026 the hash-based
route Ethereum chose gained a lattice-based rival, so the open question is less
whether such proofs are practical than which assumption pays for them.

- [`a16z/aerie`](https://github.com/a16z/aerie) — aggregates Falcon-512 or Falcon-1024 signatures into one proof with a lattice SNARK built on Akita, with no recursion. The [announcement](https://a16zfrontier.com/posts/article/fast-post-quantum-signature-aggregation-with-lattice-snarks) (October 2026) reports 32,768 Falcon-512 signatures proved in about 0.65 s on a laptop CPU into a 66 KiB proof, and about 29 times leanVM's XMSS aggregation throughput, measured by the authors. A research release by its own README, for Falcon.
- [Flock](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/1329) — a hash-based SNARK over binary fields for large batches of SHA-256, Keccak and BLAKE3 evaluations, the workload that standard-hash aggregation creates. It reports more than 660,000 BLAKE3 compressions per second on ten cores and SHA-256 proving more than 9 times faster than Binius64. A paper with a proof-of-concept implementation, not a library.
- [Lattice Jolt](https://a16zfrontier.com/posts/article/lattice-snarks-jolt-post-quantum-faster) — the [Jolt](https://github.com/a16z/jolt) RISC-V zkVM with its elliptic-curve commitment (Dory) replaced by [Akita](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/1983), a lattice polynomial commitment from [LayerZero](https://github.com/LayerZero-Labs/akita) resting on Module-SIS, so binding no longer depends on a pairing. Released September 2026, it claims proofs under 100 KB and a prover 2 to 3 times faster than Dory-based Jolt; since October its lattice parameters target NIST category 3 (ML-DSA-65), while Fiat–Shamir soundness stays at 128 bits and is proved in the classical, not quantum, random oracle model. Akita is an opt-in `akita` build feature, Dory remains Jolt's default, and the two cannot be combined with zero knowledge: Akita proves correct execution only.


## Discovery and inventory

Migration begins before any algorithm is chosen: the EU roadmap wants national
inventories started by end-2026, NCSC wants discovery done by 2028, and OMB
M-26-15 wants every US federal agency's inventory in a cryptographic bill of
materials, and all three assume an organisation can say where RSA and the elliptic curves actually are.
That is a different activity from the sections above, and in practice the hard
part is not the scan but trusting its gaps.

- [`g1mn/pqproof`](https://github.com/g1mn/pqproof) — an offline command-line tool and GitHub Action that runs the same scanners and rules over a codebase before and after a post-quantum migration, writes a per-location CBOM table (CSV, not CycloneDX) for both trees, and, given the address of a running server, checks that it negotiates X25519MLKEM768. It earns a place here because it checks the end of a migration rather than the start: classical public-key code left in the converted tree fails the run unless it is the classical half of a hybrid or an allowlist entry records why, a check that could not run is reported as undecided rather than passed, and the run ends in a hashed evidence record (RFC 8785, SHA-256) that `pqproof --verify` recomputes from the files. It reads code without running it and calls no AI model. MIT.
- [`johnInarti/pqc-readiness-action`](https://github.com/johnInarti/pqc-readiness-action) — a GitHub Action that statically scans a repository for RSA, ECDSA, ECDH/X25519, Ed25519/Ed448, finite-field Diffie-Hellman and DSA, including committed SSH and X.509 key material and manifest dependencies that implement them, and writes a CycloneDX 1.6 CBOM (ECMA-424). It earns a place here for being honest about the limit that makes inventories dangerous: a static scan cannot see dynamic loading, vendored binaries or hardware modules, so zero findings means nothing matched rather than nothing is there, and the tool prints a warning and a run annotation whenever its own coverage was truncated. Not a compliance certification. Runs in the caller's runner with no network egress under default settings, and has no third-party dependencies. Apache-2.0.
- [`moncef00327-cloud/pq-tls-action`](https://github.com/moncef00327-cloud/pq-tls-action) — a GitHub Action that asks a deployed host which TLS 1.3 key exchange it actually negotiates, and can fail the job when X25519MLKEM768 is missing or the grade drops below a threshold. It earns a place here as the network-side counterpart to a repository scan: what clients get is decided by the CDN, load balancer or origin in front of the code, which a static scan never sees. The check runs through the free public Quantum Aether scan API (no key); the `base-url` input can point it at a self-hosted copy of the open-source scanner instead.
- [`StanimirTenev/qrp-mcp`](https://github.com/StanimirTenev/qrp-mcp) — an offline post-quantum readiness scanner for code, run from the command line (`uvx qrp-mcp scan .`) or as an MCP server, that writes a CycloneDX CBOM. It earns a place here because the CBOM records what the scan read and what it did not read, so the inventory carries its own coverage alongside the findings. Runs locally, with no account and no network calls. Apache-2.0.

## Verification

Awesome lists rot. This one records how and when it was checked so you can judge how much to trust it.

**Method.** GitHub projects were resolved through the GitHub API, confirming the repository exists and is not archived, and recording its last-push date; renames and transfers were followed to their current canonical names. Non-GitHub links were fetched and required HTTP 200 after redirects. For standards documents, the claim was checked against the document itself, not against a search summary.

**Last checked: 2026-10-06.** Every GitHub entry resolved and none was archived; all had been pushed within the last five months, most within days, except `ietf-wg-pquip/state-of-protocols-and-pqc` (June 2025), which is flagged inline above rather than quietly listed alongside actively maintained projects. Of the non-GitHub links, all returned HTTP 200 except those on the two hosts named under Limits. Earlier checks and what each one changed are in the pull requests, not here.

**What that caught.** Two entries were dropped for not existing at all. Two candidate lists turned out to be the same repository under a former name, and another had been transferred to a different owner. Most importantly, a widely repeated summary had RFC 9881 and RFC 9935 assigned to the wrong algorithms; reading the RFCs shows 9881 is ML-DSA and 9935 is ML-KEM.

**Limits.** `nsa.gov`, `cisa.gov`, `nccoe.nist.gov` and `radar.cloudflare.com` return HTTP 403 to automated clients, so their content is described but not linked as verified; `datatracker.ietf.org` (403), `pq-crystals.org` (429), since October 2026 `digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu` (403), since November 2026 `blog.google` (429, intermittently), `cyber.gov.au` (timeouts, November 2026) and `akamai.com` (403) do too, but are kept because they are the authoritative sources for what they link, confirmed through RFC Editor records where possible. HTTP 200 proves a URL resolves, not that its content is still accurate. Star counts and commit dates are deliberately not recorded per entry, because they are stale the day after they are written.

The larger limit is coverage, not liveness. The first edition of this list was
searched along five axes — existing lists, standards bodies, implementations,
protocol deployment, and learning material — and had no axis for consensus-layer
work, so it missed Ethereum's post-quantum effort entirely despite that being
one of the largest PQC deployments underway. Link checking cannot find a section
that was never written. If a whole area is missing, please open an issue.
